# Game design

The principles every game taking the core shares, read before a task changes a rule, a feature or how the computer
players play; each game's own add to them. A feature is any move, rule or power a player can choose to use.

- **The computer players never know who is a person** (core archive 87). Nothing in the logic of the *computer players*,
  nor in *what they are shown*, says whether another player is a person; they judge every player by behaviour alone, so
  the game tests everyone alike, and it makes no tells that give a person away, such as computer players that answer at
  once (core archive 114).
- **The computer players never cheat** (core archive 87 and 114). They hold nothing a player in their place could not
  see or know: no sight through what hides the game from a player, no knowledge it was not given.
- **Using a feature must pay, measured three ways** (core archive 85, 86, 89 and 114). For every feature, the
  *use-against-ignore measurement* plays *measured players* that use it where it fits, as they play, against copies that
  always use it and copies that never do, the rule itself unchanged, and *where the numbers go* shows how often each
  used it, how often it worked and who came out ahead, by more than chance (its threshold a first guess, listed). If
  those that use it where it fits do not beat both those that never and those that always do (core archive 114), the
  change says why: the feature does not matter, and is reworked or cut, or they play it badly, fixed in the same change.
  One too rare to move whole games is judged per use, each use played on with and without it; and every feature is
  measured against players who play its counter (core archive 143).
- **No feature only people use** (core archive 87, 88 and 89). Every feature a person can use, the *computer players*
  use too, where it pays and with success at least sometimes: one only people use goes untested and hands them an edge
  no rule intended. A feature is not finished until the *measured players* use it so, by the point above's numbers.
- **No rule untaught** (core archive 84 and 90). Every rule a player needs is in *where rules are taught*, added or
  changed in the same change as the rule, and when a rule acts on a player, the game shows it where and when that player
  could know it; nothing the game itself shows implies what is untrue (a player may still lie), so whatever it shows
  from the past, such as a last-known position or an old report, shows its age (core archive 114). Every mistake a
  player makes in testing is sorted by one question (core archive 112): could a careful player, seeing the same, have
  known better? If so it is the game's, and kept; if not, the game told it badly: a bug, fixed before the next test.
- **Strong computer players** (core archive 82, 83 and 90). The *computer players* play as competently as they can,
  within the limits their game sets them, never weaker on purpose (a game is made easier or harder by its setup
  instead): the rules are measured through their play, and weak play hides what is wrong with a rule. Stronger never
  means samey: where there are several, each keeps its own style, checked before and after a change to their play.
- **Don't prohibit; give it its real cost** (core archive 91 and 92), so the player keeps its agency. An action a player
  could realistically take is never forbidden because it seems unwise; the game's own rules make it as costly, risky and
  rare as it really was, so the rare exception stays possible, as Hannibal crossed the Alps.
- **No solutions, only trade-offs** (core archive 91 and 93). Every feature helps in some situations and costs in
  others: none is always right or always wrong to use, nor makes a player safe (core archive 143), as the measurement
  above checks.
- **Reality first** (core archive 102 and 104). Where the real world, or the history a game is set in, is interesting
  enough on its own, the game takes it as it was and invents nothing in its place; invention fills only what reality
  leaves silent, or what play cannot bear, and only after trying to keep both.
- **Effects from causes, as deep as pays** (core archive 102 to 104). Every effect comes from a cause, and the deeper
  and better grounded the cause, the more compelling the outcome, even where a player never sees every link: a quantity
  follows what drives it, never a flat share; what goes wrong for a player comes from a cause it could reason about,
  chance standing in only for causes too small to model; nothing appears or vanishes without a cause it could name.
  Depth stops where more simulation no longer pays for what it costs to build and run. Depth underneath costs a player
  no attention; what a player must follow is kept readable by teaching and showing it, never by making the simulation
  shallower (core archive 114).
- **Levers, not luck** (core archive 110 and 111). Where chance enters, players have levers to prepare for it or answer
  it, so no outcome is decided by chance alone.
- **Different data, same rules** (core archive 105). No rule checks which player, faction or character it is: what sets
  one apart lives in its data and starting conditions, which every rule reads alike, never in a rule that names it; only
  presentation and voice may name one.
- **The order of resolution never decides** (core archive 105). When two act at the same moment, the outcome does not
  depend on which the game settles first: they share, or a seeded draw decides.
- **Everything new belongs** (core archive 105). Every new feature feels balanced, cohesive and purposeful, and fits the
  rest of the game: it reads and changes quantities other systems use, never keeping its own beside them (core archive
  114).
- **No stopgaps** (core archive 107). The work serves the finished game, so nothing is built for the meantime: where a
  change comes before a feature it would want, the game stays as it is until that feature comes, and the change says so.
  A lever is built with the counterweight that answers it, never with a stand-in for it, one moved in the order of work
  if need be.
- **Numbers are first guesses until play tunes them** (core archive 86 and 108). A number a change sets that neither
  reality nor its causes fix (a time, a cost, a chance) is a first guess, listed in the change and tuned by measured
  play, never just picked. While the systems that change its effect are still being built it stays untuned, and a tuning
  pass takes them together once they stand; anything that makes the game unplayable for testing is fixed at once.
- **Code decides facts; models phrase them,** in a game with language-model players or characters (core archive 105).
  The game's code decides what is true and what each seat learns; a model adds only voice, personality and judgement
  within its own seat. A model is called only where its judgement can change what happens; the obvious, the routine and
  the mechanical are the code's (core archive 112).
- **In a game played online, everything new works online** (core archive 106), the same on every screen, with the
  protocol's version raised when its messages change.
- **Players always play the newest version** (core archive 109, 115 and 117). Opening the game starts its newest
  version; where it cannot load itself anew, the game says a newer one is out, with one click to play it. A version that
  only fixes or looks different takes over by itself as soon as it interrupts no game in play (between rounds, at a turn
  of the game's clock, or at the next launch); one that changes play waits until the game in play ends or the game is
  next opened. A saved game opened in a newer version carries on in the newer rules, and its log marks where they
  changed.
- **Same seed, same game** (core archive 112 and 114). A game replays exactly from its seed within one version of its
  rules, and where people or language models play, from its log of every input and every model answer, the model and its
  prompts counting as part of the version: a change that should change no play proves it by the same seeds and logs; an
  optimization that changes decisions proves, on fresh seeds, that nothing a player would notice did (skill, styles,
  feel and look, within noise; core archive 143); and one that changes play says what it changes and starts a new
  version. A game whose rules changed while it was played replays exactly only from the change on.
- **Readable by every player** (core archive 112). Nothing is told apart by colour alone, reduced motion keeps whatever
  informs, and each thing has one name everywhere the game shows it.
