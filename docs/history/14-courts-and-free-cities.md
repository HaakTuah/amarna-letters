# 14. Courts and free cities

For item 14's echo "A new Idrimi", item 16 ("The royal council": four advisors, with "Period titles per court ... to
be verified before use") and item 17 ("How cities become free", "Revolt follows conditions, not dice", "A head rises
where men lack one"). Grades and abbreviations: [README](README.md); works this dossier adds are listed at the end. Who
ruled where is in [01-politics.md](01-politics.md), the people by court in [06-attested-people.md](06-attested-people.md),
the vassals' grievances in [09-the-letters.md](09-the-letters.md), commanders and garrisons in
[11-armies-and-fleets.md](11-armies-and-fleets.md) ("Command"), and sieges in
[12-walls-roads-harbours.md](12-walls-roads-harbours.md) ("Sieges on record"); this file links to them rather than
repeating them.

**In one line:** every Great King ruled in person and alone in law, but each was surrounded by a few great officers,
a queen or queen mother and his own brothers and sons, who advised, carried his word and, at a death or a defeat, made
or unmade kings; and in the Levant of the letters cities changed hands far more often by turning than by storm: a
town killed its lord or shut its gate on him when hunger, an absent master and a neighbour's promise came together.

## Courts in 1346, power by power

What each office did, and how much it could check the king. The council of item 16 is four advisors who only inform
("The royal council"); the real officers below are people, so they belong to the character pool (item 10, "Attested
characters"), not to the council.

**How far the court could check him** [B]: in law not at all. In practice the king's word was carried and shaped by
the vizier, the chamberlain and the commissioners, and the vassals worked through them (Tutu for Aziru, EA 158, 164) [A]. What the court could not
stop in a king's life it undid after it: the Restoration Stela reopens Amun's temples under the boy Tutankhamun [A], steered,
most think, by Ay and Horemheb [B].

<a id="14-egypt-succession"></a>**Succession** [C]: after Akhenaten (about 1336) came a short, disputed time of Smenkhkare and of a female king
Neferneferuaten (Nefertiti or Meritaten, scholars divide), then Tutankhamun (about 1332), then Ay, then Horemheb, the
general: the last two of these rulers (Smenkhkare and Neferneferuaten may be one) rose from his court's offices, not his blood [A]. A widowed queen of Egypt
asked Suppiluliuma for a son to marry, since "I will never take a servant of mine as husband"; the prince Zannanza was
killed on the way [A: the request in *Deeds*, fragment 28; the death in a later fragment of the *Deeds*, Mursili II's
second Plague Prayer and Suppiluliuma's letter KUB 19.20; who the queen was, Ankhesenamun most often, is C].

**How far the court could check him** [B]: the old assembly, the *panku*, had judged murders in the royal family and
could even try a king's kin (the Proclamation of Telipinu, about 1500) [A], but it hardly appears in the empire's texts,
so by 1346 the check was informal: the Great Ones advised, the oracles could say no, and the oath bound them to him and
his heir [B]. The Proclamation also fixed the succession: a first-rank son, else a second-rank son, else a husband of a
first-rank daughter [A].

**Intrigue and succession on record:**
- **Suppiluliuma's own rise** [A, C his part]: the princes, lords and officers had sworn to Tudhaliya the Younger, then
  "wronged" and killed him, and Suppiluliuma reigned; his son Mursili II later blamed the plague on that broken oath
  (Mursili's first Plague Prayer, CTH 378). Whether Suppiluliuma was Tudhaliya's brother or rival is C.
- <a id="14-hatti-after"></a>**After him** [A]: Arnuwanda II reigned about a year and died of the plague; the boy Mursili II opens his *Annals*
  with the enemies who mocked a child king.

**Intrigue and succession on record:**
- <a id="14-ud-hi"></a>**A regent who killed a king** [A, B that he ruled]: when Tushratta came to the throne young, one UD-hi (the name's reading is C)
  "had done an unseemly thing" and killed his lord, Tushratta's brother Artashumara, and kept the young king from friends;
  Tushratta then killed the slayers (EA 17 (Moran): "UD-hi had done an unseemly thing to my country and had slain his lord").
- <a id="14-artatama"></a><a id="14-shuttarna"></a>**Two kings at once** [A, B the timing]: Artatama II calls himself "king of the Hurrians" against Tushratta, with
  Hittite backing, and his son Shuttarna III later hands the palace treasure to Assyria and Alshe and gives them the
  Mitanni nobles to impale (the Shattiwaza treaty, Beckman no. 6A-B; Luckenbill's KBo I 3: "Shuttarna, son of Artatama, [king of Harri" ... "He caused the nobles to be taken to Assyria and Alshe").
- <a id="14-shattiwaza"></a>**Murdered by his son** [A]: "his son, together with his servants, killed his father Tushratta", a few years after
  1346 (Beckman no. 6A). Another son, Shattiwaza, fled, found no help in Babylonia by his own account, came to
  Suppiluliuma, married his daughter and was put back on the throne by a Hittite army under Piyassili [A, by the
  winner's account; Luckenbill's KBo I 1: "he slew his father Tushratta"].
- <a id="14-akit-teshup"></a>**A noble who left with his chariots** [A]: Akit-Teshup fled to Babylonia with chariotry (Beckman no. 6B; Luckenbill's KBo I 3: "With two hundred chariots he fled").
- **Gifts as standing** [B]: Tushratta's complaint that Akhenaten sent gold-plated wooden statues instead of the solid
  gold ones promised (EA 27, 29) is about his standing before his own court as much as about gold [A the complaint].

**Intrigue and succession on record:**
- <a id="14-kurigalzu-refusal"></a>**A king who refused another king's vassals** [A]: Burna-Buriash tells Pharaoh that in Kurigalzu's day ("my father" in
  Rainey, "my ancestor" in Moran) Pharaoh's Canaanite vassals asked Babylon to join them against Egypt, and Kurigalzu
  refused (EA 9; Rainey: "all the Canaanites wrote to him, saying: 'Come to the border of the land; let us revolt'"). A king could turn
  away a revolt offered to him.
- <a id="14-nazi-bugash"></a>**The army raises a "son of nobody"** [A, C the names]: Kara-hardash, Ashur-uballit's daughter's son and Burna-Buriash's
  son or grandson (whether she married Burna-Buriash or one of his sons is C), was killed when the Kassite troops rose; they raised Nazi-Bugash, "a son of nobody"; Ashur-uballit
  marched, killed him and put Kurigalzu II on the throne (Synchronistic History; Chronicle P gives other names and
  details). After 1346, about 1333. Synchronistic History (ABC 21, i 8'-17', [livius](https://www.livius.org/sources/content/mesopotamian-chronicles-content/abc-21-synchronic-chronicle/)):
  "They appointed Nazibugaš, a Kassite, son of a nobody, as sovereign over them"; "Kurigalzu the Younger, son of
  Burnaburiaš, he appointed as king" (livius dates the revolt to 1323 on its own reckoning [C]).

### Arzawa

The court is almost silent [S]: in Amenhotep III's day a king (Tarhundaradu, to whom EA 31 is written) answers in Hittite through a scribe who adds a
personal note to Pharaoh's scribe (EA 32) [A]. The Arzawa lands were ruled by their own lords, often kin to each other and often at war
over the throne [B: Bryce, Kingdom], so Arzawa supplies the clearest cases of exiles a Great King sheltered and used:
Mashhuiluwa, brought back to Mira, and Madduwatta, given new land instead (below).

### The city-states

- <a id="14-abdi-heba"></a>**A vassal ruled by his overlord's word** [A]: Abdi-Heba of Jerusalem: "neither my father nor my mother put me in this
  place, but the strong arm of the king brought me into my father's house" (EA 286 (Moran); also EA 287-288). Sons of
  vassals were held or raised at the overlord's court: the son of Aki-Teshup, whom the citizens of Tunip ask back from
  Pharaoh (EA 59) [A]; Aziru himself was held in Egypt while a son (EA 169, Moran's reading) and Baaluya, perhaps his
  brother, wrote for him (EA 170) [A, B who they were].
- **Elders and citizens** [A]: some towns write with no king at all, as "Tunip and its citizens" (EA 59) and "Irqata and
  its elders" (EA 100). These are the people who decide whether a gate opens.
- **Brothers** [A]: the ruler's brothers are his likeliest rivals: Rib-Hadda's younger brother at Byblos (EA 137-138, 162), Akit-Teshup at Niya
  (Beckman no. 6A), the sons of Abdi-Ashirta sharing Amurru (EA 104, 132).
- **Ugarit** [A, later]: its 13th-century texts show a prefect (*sakinu*), a queen mother with her own seal and
  correspondence, and scribes; a young king Arhalba was soon replaced by his brother Niqmepa under Hittite eyes [B]
  (Van Soldt).

**Start from:** use a title only where the row says A or B; elsewhere use the plain archetype name ("the General",
"the Steward") rather than invent one [G]. For city-states, the plain names throughout [G].

**Advice on record**, for the council's voice (style, never strategy): Suppiluliuma calling the Great Ones and then
sending to check before acting (*Deeds*, fragment 28) [A]; the oracle questions, which put a plan as a yes-or-no and
ask again if the answer is unclear [A]; the vizier's morning report (Duties of the Vizier) [A]. All three inform and
leave the choice to the king, as item 16's advisors do.

**Omens of the end** (item 16): kings read their own fall in signs and blamed broken oaths: Mursili II's Plague Prayers
lay twenty years of plague on Suppiluliuma's broken oath and on Hatti's breach of its treaty with Egypt [A]; Mursili's
annals record a sign in the sky, often read as the solar eclipse of about 1312 [C]. Style only.

| <a id="14-byblos"></a><a id="14-ili-rapih"></a>Byblos | about 1340 | Rib-Hadda went to Ammunira at Beirut for help; his younger brother (unnamed in the letters; most identify him with Ili-rapih, who writes EA 139-140 [B], a son of Rib-Hadda in one study [C]) swore to the city, "the lords of the city" joined the sons of Abdi-Ashirta, and they would not let him in; he never got it back, and is last heard of at Sidon, where Aziru took him and "gave him to (some) mayors" (EA 162) | Hunger (years of it); the ruler absent; a brother as the head; the master's archers never came | A | EA 136-138, 139-140, 162; EA 137 (Moran): "my younger brother turned Gubla into an enemy"; Rainey, introduction: "The brother who had ousted Rib-Haddi was Ilirapi'" |
| <a id="14-byblos-hunger"></a>Byblos's people before | earlier | An attempt on Rib-Hadda's life; peasants (*hupšu*) ready to desert to the Habiru; children sold for grain | Hardship as the slow cause | A | EA 74-75, 81, 85, 118; EA 81 (Moran): "A man with a bronze dagger ... [attacked m]e" |

**What the record says** [B]: the letters describe no assault by Amurru's men: many towns are simply "taken"
(Shigata, Ampi, Batruna, Sumur: EA 76-91, 104-106), and where they say how, it is a killing or a gate opened from within, after months or years of blockade and hunger, while the master's archers do not come
(Abdi-Heba: "if there are no archers this year, all the lands of the king are lost", EA 286-288) [A]. That is "the gate
opened from within" ("How long a siege lasts") and "Revolt follows conditions, not dice" at once.

**Start from:** a besieged or starving city's gate opens from within only after **long** hunger: Byblos held for
years with its people selling their children (EA 74-75), so take the long end, no sooner than the stores of
[12-walls-roads-harbours.md](12-walls-roads-harbours.md) run out (two to four seasons), and later where its ruler is
present and loved [G]. **A ruler absent** from his city weighs heavily: Rib-Hadda lost Byblos the one time he left it
[A, one case: G as a weight].

**What the record says** [B]: the new Idrimi, in the sources, needs three things together: a band of men loyal to him
rather than to a king (Habiru, an army kept, the levies a governor raised), a land where the old master is weak or
absent, and in the end an overlord to recognise him: Idrimi and Aziru both made their peace with a Great King once
they ruled. Item 17 builds the first two; the third is already the vassal diplomacy of item 11+.

**Start from:** a rise takes **years, not seasons**: Idrimi's "seven years" twice are a formula [C], but every case
spans years (Abdi-Ashirta's rise, Aziru's, Labaya's war) [A]; start from the long end for a band growing into a
city's ruler, and the short end (a season or two) for a governor or commander who already holds levies or an army
[G]. "A new Idrimi" in the chronicle (item 14) fits both: Idrimi is the band case, Madduwatta the granted-land case.

**What the record says** [B]: a living exile was a standing cause for as long as he lived, and kings kept exiles as a

### The Habiru

- **Not a people** [B]: *'apiru* (written SA.GAZ) were men who had left their town and lord: runaways, debtors,
  outlaws and hired fighters, found from Anatolia to Egypt and across five centuries (Rainey; Na'aman). Idrimi lived
  among them; Biryawaza counts "my Habiru" among his troops (EA 195) [A].
- **"Becoming Habiru"** [A]: in the letters a town or a peasant *becomes* Habiru by leaving Pharaoh's order (EA 74, 85,
  288). For the game this is a band ("Bands, pirates and outlaws"): men who leave their lord and act together, the
  ground a head rises from ("A head rises where men lack one").
- **Hunger feeds them** [A]: Rib-Hadda's peasants threaten to go over for grain (EA 85, 118); see
  [11-armies-and-fleets.md](11-armies-and-fleets.md) ("Logistics") on unfed men going over.

### Home provinces

A Great King's own land rose rarely [B]. Hatti's near-collapse under Tudhaliya III came from enemies on every side, the
Kaska and Arzawa, not from Hittite provinces breaking away (KBo VI 28) [A]; at home the record shows coups at the
capital (Babylon about 1333, Mitanni twice, Hatti's Tudhaliya the Younger) rather than provinces seceding [B]. That
fits item 17's "absorbed land starts loyal and needs far more to rise"; a coup at the capital is a different thing,
which the game does not model and this dossier does not ask for.

### Armies living off the land (item 17)

- **Troops taking food anger the land** [A]: Abdi-Heba complains that Pharaoh's own Nubian (Kashi) troops in Jerusalem
  "attempted a very serious crime" against his house, and asks that any troops sent be provided for, lest the land be
  "in dire straits" on their account (EA 287, Moran); vassals stocked grain, oxen and sheep "for the archers of the king" so they need not take it
  (EA 55, 141-142, 320-326). The detail is in [11-armies-and-fleets.md](11-armies-and-fleets.md) ("Logistics").
- **Grain that did not come** [A]: Rib-Hadda disputes Yanhamu's claim to have given him grain ("What did he give me?",
  EA 85) and asks for Yarimuta's grain (EA 83, 85-86). That commanders kept such grain for themselves no letter says:
  the commander "who may keep any money meant for food, and just take instead" is the game's own [G], not the letters'.

### Grievances held by the dispossessed

A ruler whose land was taken remembered it and said so for years: Rib-Hadda's sixty letters against Amurru (EA
68-138); Shuwardata and Abdi-Heba over Qiltu (EA 280, 289); Ayyab against Hazor (EA 364); Akizzi of Qatna against
Aitakama (EA 53-55); Aitakama against Biryawaza for burning his towns (EA 189) [A]. The vassal grievances are tabled in
[09-the-letters.md](09-the-letters.md) ("Among the vassals"). **Start from:** a grievance lasts while its holder lives
[G, from the letters: none of these is ever withdrawn].

## The conditions of "Revolt follows conditions, not dice", against the record

Which cases show each condition, and which weight to start strongest. All weights are [G] for item 17's first guesses;
balance waits for item 18.

| Condition | Shown by | Start from |
| --- | --- | --- |
| How new the conquest | Sumur lost and retaken; Nuhashshe and Niya turning back against Hatti after the one-year campaign (Beckman no. 6A-7); Tette of Nuhashshe rebelling against Mursili II [A] | Strong at first, as the intent says |
| Hardship (hunger, levies, gold taken, troops taking food) | Byblos, Lachish, the peasants going over (EA 74-75, 85, 118, 287-288) | The strongest single weight in the letters |
| The master's weakness | "If there are no archers this year, the lands are lost" (EA 286-288); Sumur taken while its garrison was away | Next strongest; with hardship, the pair that turns a town |
| Distance | Egypt's far vassals in Amurru and the north turned; those near Gaza mostly held [B] | Weak by itself |
| A cause to rally to | Exiles returning (above); Labaya's sons carrying on; brothers taking the throne | Strong while the old ruler lives |
| Outside help | Abdi-Ashirta's "kill your lord" (EA 74); Aziru's envoys; Hatti's pressure on Qatna and Qadesh (EA 53-55) | Strong, and always denied |
| What lowers it: troops, a loyal governor, a good harvest | Megiddo held with Biridiya present and asking for 100 men (EA 244); towns that stocked the archers' grain stayed loyal [B] | As the intent says |

## What item 17 should not read into this

- **No rule names a city.** The cases are Levantine because the letters are; the rule is the same for every province
  ("Different data, same rules").
- **Style, not strategy.** That Amurru rose does not mean the game's Amurru should want to; the record only says what
  conditions turned towns.
- **Partisan sources.** Most of the accusations are one side's word (Rib-Hadda, Abdi-Heba); they show what was feared
  and believed, which is what a king hears from his governors too.
