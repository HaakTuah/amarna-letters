# 11. Armies and fleets

For "Command at a distance" and the armies and fleets each power starts with (item 13, "Starting positions come from
the sources"), and the pace of war the balance pass tunes (item 18). Grades and abbreviations: [README](README.md); the
works this dossier adds are under [Sources](#sources-this-dossier-adds). Map ids are those of
`src/engine/src/model/map.ts`. The soldiers' share of the people, and the vassals' requests for a few dozen men, are in
[04-people-and-settlement.md](04-people-and-settlement.md) ("Who people were"); the commanders and officials by name are
in [06-attested-people.md](06-attested-people.md); journey times are in [02-geography.md](02-geography.md).

**In one line:** a Great King's field army was of thousands to a few tens of thousands, built round chariots and archers,
raised for a campaign each spring and sent home or into garrisons each autumn; a vassal city held tens to a few hundreds;
no power kept a war fleet as such, and ships were merchantmen and transports pressed into service, most of them owned by
the coastal cities.

## How sure the numbers are

No document of the period counts a whole army. The figures come from victory accounts (Thutmose III's *Annals*, the
Qadesh poem and reliefs, Mursili II's *Annals*), from treaties fixing a vassal's contingent, from the rosters and ration
lists of Nuzi, Alalakh and Ugarit, and from what the Amarna letters ask for. Victory accounts inflate the enemy and
round the booty [B: the handbooks agree]; rosters and treaties are the soundest. So every size below is a range graded
[C] unless a document gives it, and the start values are proportions between powers, which is what the game needs
first: item 13 turns them into its own unit of strength.

## Command

- <a id="11-kings-led"></a>**Kings led in person** on the great campaigns: Thutmose III at Megiddo, Suppiluliuma in his one-year campaign,
  Mursili II every year of his *Annals* [A]. A king's absence was a risk at home: Mursili's *Annals* open with the
  enemies who mocked him as a child king [A].
- <a id="11-commanders"></a>**Princes and great officials led the rest:** Suppiluliuma's sons Telipinu (Kizzuwatna, then Aleppo) and Piyassili
  (Sharri-Kushuh, at Carchemish) commanded in Syria as viceroys [A: *Deeds*; Beckman]; in Egypt the commissioners
  (*rabisu*) such as Yanhamu led the archers sent into Canaan and handed out grain (see the people dossier) [A: EA 86 (Moran): "Yanhamu sent you grain"; EA 286, of the king's garrison: "Enhamu has taken ... away"]; the
  Hittite border governors (*bel madgalti*) held the frontier forts and their garrisons by written instructions [A:
  Miller, *Royal Hittite Instructions*].
- <a id="11-vassal-contingents"></a>**A vassal led his own men**, and the treaties bound him to bring them when his overlord campaigned near, or to come
  in person (Beckman: the treaties with Sunashshura, Aziru and Tette of Nuhashshe) [A]. Egypt's vassals were told to make ready for its archers, with food
  (below) and with their own horses and chariots (EA 141-142; EA 195) [A]: EA 142 (Moran): "I have indeed made preparations, including my
  horses and ((and)) chariots".

## Logistics and the campaigning season

- <a id="11-campaigning-season"></a>**Spring to autumn.** Thutmose III left Tjaru in the fourth month of *Peret* (about April) and fought at Megiddo in
  May [A: *Annals*; Breasted, *Ancient Records* II § 415: "Year 22, fourth month of the second season"]; Mursili II campaigned each year from spring and went home, or sometimes wintered in the field, as
  in Arzawa [A: his *Annals*]. Winter closed the Anatolian plateau and the Taurus passes [B: see the environment
  dossier]. **Reading [G]:** a campaign is a season or two a year, as the game's seasons already make it; an army kept
  in the field through winter costs its pay and food and its sick, not a rule against it.
- <a id="11-supply"></a>**Food went ahead of the army.** Thutmose III had the Phoenician harbours stocked with loaves, oil, wine and honey,
  recorded in his campaigns from his seventh (year 31) on [A: *Annals*; Breasted, *Ancient Records* II § 472: "every harbor at which
  his majesty arrived was supplied with ... loaves"]. The vassals write that they have made ready food, drink, oil,
  grain, oxen and sheep for the king's troops (Yidya of Ashkelon, EA 324-325; Akizzi of Qatna, EA 55) [A]: EA 324 (Moran):
  "I have indeed prepared food, strong drink, oil, grain, oxen, sheep and goats". Troops quartered on a land were a
  burden the vassals feared [A: Abdi-Heba of his Kashite troops, EA 287 (Moran): "all of the land might be in dire straits
  on their account"].
- <a id="11-rations"></a>**Rations and burden** [B: Engels, *Alexander the Great and the Logistics of the Macedonian Army*, for the
  arithmetic]: a soldier eats about 1.4 kg (3 lb) of grain a day, a horse about 4.5 kg of grain and as much again of forage where it cannot
  graze; a donkey carries perhaps 70-90 kg [G]. So an army of 10,000 with 1,000 chariots (about 2,500 horses) needs about 25
  tonnes of grain a day, besides forage, which is why armies kept to rivers, harbours and the stores of the towns they crossed.
- <a id="11-pay"></a>**Pay.** Soldiers were paid in rations and land; hired men in silver and goods [A: the sons of Labayu "have given their
  money to the 'Apiru and to the Suteans" (EA 246, Moran); Egyptian soldiers' plots in the Wilbour Papyrus, later]. Rib-Hadda
  writes that his people's sons, daughters and household goods have been sold in Yarimuta for food (EA 74, 75, 81, 85) [A:
  EA 75 (Moran): "sons and daughters and the furnishings of the houses are gone"], and the same letters show unfed
  peasants going over to the Habiru (EA 74, 81) [A].

## Fleets

No power of the period kept a war fleet as later navies did. Ships were owned by kings, temples and merchants and pressed
into service as transports and, rarely, for fights at sea [B: Wachsmann].

<a id="11-uluburun"></a>**Ships themselves** [A: the wrecks]: the Uluburun ship (late 14th century) was about 15 m long and carried about 20 tonnes of
cargo, with a crew of perhaps 5-10 [G: the excavator finds the evidence does not show the crew's size]; the Cape Gelidonya ship (about 1200) about 10 m. Larger merchantmen are implied by
cargoes of grain reckoned in thousands of measures (RS 20.212) [C]. A ship of war, when ships fought, was such a ship
with soldiers aboard: archers and spearmen fighting from deck as at Medinet Habu (about 1177) [A, a century and a half
later]. **Reading [G]:** a fleet's strength is its ships and the men aboard, and its main work is carrying armies and
grain and keeping another's ships from a coast; when it can sail is in the environment dossier ("The sea").

## What item 13 should not read into this

The letters ask for troops as vassals ask a lord, in a voice of need ("Style, not strategy" in the README): that
Rib-Hadda begs for 300 men is a fact about Byblos's weakness, not a rule that Egypt should send them.
