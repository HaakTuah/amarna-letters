# 15. Pirates, bandits and tolls

For item 15 ("Infrastructure and policing": "policing, patrols and garrisons whose cost holds banditry and piracy down
and catches smugglers at the borders", with "Pirates and bandits" and "Tolls and smuggling") and the outlaws item 11
built ("Bands, pirates and outlaws"). Grades and abbreviations: [README](README.md); works this dossier adds are listed
at the end. Roads, their upkeep, relay stations, the Hittite border governors and harbours are in
[12-walls-roads-harbours.md](12-walls-roads-harbours.md); garrisons, hired men and fleets in
[11-armies-and-fleets.md](11-armies-and-fleets.md); the Habiru's politics and how towns "became Habiru" in
[14-courts-and-free-cities.md](14-courts-and-free-cities.md) ("The Habiru"); the routes in
[02-geography.md](02-geography.md); the grievances as a voice in [09-the-letters.md](09-the-letters.md). This file links
to them rather than repeating them.

**In one line:** robbery of caravans and envoys is on record across the Levant, and the king whose land it happened in
was held answerable, by his peers' demands and, a century later, by written rules that made the town where a merchant
died pay for him; piracy in 1346 means the Lukka raiding Alashiya and the coastal towns seizing one another's ships;
the Habiru and the Suteans are the road's bandits; tolls are attested as something a king could waive for a friend,
smuggling only outside the period; and policing was the Medjay in Egypt, watch posts under the Hittite governors and
tens of soldiers in a vassal's town, whose cost no source counts.

## How sure this is

The Amarna letters (1360-1332) are the period's own evidence, and they are one-sided: every robbery in them is a
grievance written to win something, compensation, troops or a rival's ruin [B]. Most of the rules on merchants and
tolls come from Ugarit's archives and the Hittite laws, a century later (Ugarit, about 1250-1190) or earlier (the
Hittite laws, from about 1600, copied and revised through the period) [A for the texts]. Smuggling is best attested
five centuries earlier, in the Old Assyrian merchants' letters from Kanesh (about 1900-1850) [A]. Where a later or
earlier source carries the point, the grade says so: **[A, later]** means a primary source attests it, but not for
1346. Figures for the cost of policing do not survive for any court [S]; every number of that kind here is [G].

**What the cases share** [B]: a Great King held the lord of the land answerable for what happened on its roads, whoever
had done it, and wrote to him, not to the robber. That is the game's rule already ("A road belongs to the land it
crosses"): the holder of a province rules its roads, its policing and its tolls. Two further things the record shows:

- <a id="15-lords-own-men"></a>**The robbers were often the lord's own men** [A]. Biryawaza was Pharaoh's man at Damascus and Kumidi, Pamahu is
  called his governor (EA 7), and of the killers at Hinnatuna Shutatna was the son of Surata, king of Akko, and later
  ruled it himself (EA 8, 232-235), while Shum-Adda is often taken for Shamu-Adda of Shamhuna, another vassal (EA 225)
  [B] (Moran, EA 8, n. 3: the scribe "interchanged the vowels in the first syllable of the names of the two rulers"). Abdi-Heba writes that soldiers of Pharaoh's garrison, the Kashi, nearly killed him in his own house (EA 287) [A;
  B that they were Nubians: Moran's index takes "Kashite" as the gentilic of Kashi, Nubia, and Rainey reads Cushites]. Robbery by the
  powerful, then denial, is the normal case, which the game already allows ("Deniable attacks").
- <a id="15-compensation-demand"></a>**The demand was compensation and punishment, rarely war** [A]. Burna-Buriash asks for the silver back and the
  killers dead, and warns that unpunished robbery will cut the messengers off (EA 8); no letter shows a war fought over
  a robbed caravan [S]. **Reading [G]:** a robbed envoy or caravan is a grievance between the two courts, settled in
  words and silver, which item 15 needs no rule for: the game's envoys, tablets and gifts carry it.

**What this gives the game** [B]: the norm that the land where a foreign merchant dies pays for him, by catching the
killers or, failing that, out of the town's own silver, held from Burna-Buriash's demand (EA 8) to Ugarit's edicts. In
1346 it was a norm between kings, enforced by letters and by the Hittite king over his own vassals, not a rule any
court could compel [B]. **Reading [G]:** item 15 builds no automatic compensation; a king who loses men abroad may ask
for silver, and the holder may pay, refuse or blame the Habiru ("Deniable attacks"). If a script king ever needs a
figure for what to ask, **start from** Ugarit's 3 minas of silver for each man killed, with the goods made good, which
is what neighbours of one overlord actually agreed; the Hittite laws' 100 minas is a penalty, not a price.

## Sea raiders and piracy

### Alashiya and the Lukka

- <a id="15-lukka"></a>**The Lukka raided Alashiya year by year** [A]: "men of Lukki, year by year, seize villages in my own country" (EA 38, Moran).
  Pharaoh had accused the men of Alashiya of joining them; the king of Alashiya denies it, and offers that if any of
  his men were with them, Pharaoh should send them to him and he would deal with them (EA 38) [A]. So a coastal king
  answered for his own men at sea as a lord answered for his roads [B].
- <a id="15-madduwatta"></a>**Alashiya had been raided before** [A]: the Hittite text against Madduwatta (about 1400) charges him and Attarissiya
  "of Ahhiya" with raiding Alashiya, which the Hittite king claimed as his tributary (CTH 147; *Ahhiyawa Texts*, AhT 3, §36': "Because [the land] of Alasiya belongs to My Majesty") [A]; whether
  Attarissiya was a Mycenaean lord is argued [C].
- <a id="15-lukka-lands"></a>**Who the Lukka were** [B]: people of the coast and hills of south-western Anatolia, Lycia among them (AhT, p. 262: "the coast
  of Lycia, part of Lukka territory") [C on the bounds], never one kingdom, half
  in and half out of Hatti's order; Hittite texts name the "Lukka lands" as rebels and as a refuge [A]. On the map
  `lukka` is a lawless coast with no city (see [08-beyond-the-edges.md](08-beyond-the-edges.md) and 12, "Walls by
  city").
- <a id="15-later-raids"></a>**Later, the same waters got worse** [A, later]: Piyamaradu raided the Hittite coast and Lukka with Ahhiyawa's
  backing and carried off thousands of people (the Tawagalawa letter, AhT 4, about 1250, gives 7,000 [C on the reading]); in
  Ugarit's last years its king wrote to Alashiya of enemy ships burning his towns while his own ships were "in Lukka"
  (RS 20.238, about 1190; Bryce, *Letters*, p. 219: "all my ships are in the Land of Lukka") [A, later]. This is the collapse a century and a half after 1346, not the game's start.

### The Levant coast

- <a id="15-arwad-ships"></a>**Coastal towns seized each other's ships** [A]. Rib-Hadda of Byblos complains that the men of Arwad's ships
  attacked him (EA 101), that Sumur was besieged by Abdi-Ashirta's sons by land and "the people of Arwada by sea", that
  Arwad's ships intercepted the ships he sent and he could not send his ships to Sumur for fear of them (EA 105), and
  that Yapah-Hadda of Beirut seized a ship of his and went to sea to seize more (EA 114) [A for the complaints]. Abi-Milku
  of Tyre writes that Zimrida of Sidon seized Usu, the town on the mainland, so that "we have neither water nor wood",
  and that Zimrida and the men of Arwad gathered their ships to take Tyre (EA 148-149) [A]. Piracy here is the vassals' war by other means, by ships a lord owned, not by free raiders [B].
- <a id="15-sherden"></a>**Hired men, perhaps from the sea** [C]: men called *šerdanu* were at Byblos and were killed there by
  Pihura's Suteans (EA 122-123; see 11, "Hired men"), which suggests they served Rib-Hadda [B]; whether they were
  the Sherden is argued: Moran (index) thinks the word "probably has nothing to do with" them [C]. A century later a
  stela of Ramesses II at Tanis, praising his valour, names "the rebellious-hearted Sherden" and "ships of war in the
  midst of the [sea]" (Breasted, *ARE* III, §§488ff.; about 1278) [A, later]. In 1346 *šerdanu* appear only as
  soldiers, not as raiders [B].
- <a id="15-amenhotep-son-of-hapu"></a>**Egypt's coast** [B]: Amenhotep son of Hapu, Amenhotep III's scribe of recruits, says on his Karnak statue that he
  set troops at the heads of the roads to turn back the foreigners and watched the Nile's mouths with them (Urk. IV;
  Cumming; Breasted, *ARE* II, §916: "I placed troops at the heads of the way(s) to turn back the foreigners") [A for the text; B for reading it as a coastal guard]. No source of the period shows Egyptian warships
  hunting pirates [S].

## Bandits on the roads: the Habiru and the Suteans

The Habiru as a social fact, men who had left their lord, and how a town "became Habiru", are in
[14-courts-and-free-cities.md](14-courts-and-free-cities.md) ("The Habiru"). Here only what they did on the roads.

- <a id="15-habiru"></a>**The vassals ask to be saved from them, with robbers and Suteans** [A]: Dagan-takala writes to Pharaoh, "Save me
  from the powerful enemies, from the hand of the 'Apiru, robbers, and Suteans" (EA 318, Moran). The robbers
  (*habbatu*) are a word of their own beside the Habiru [A]; whether they are one group is argued [C].
- <a id="15-suteans"></a>**The Suteans** [A]: by this period the name "designated roaming and often dangerous bands" (Moran, index), nomads
  of the Syrian steppe [B], who appear in the letters as hired fighters (Biryawaza's "my Suteans", EA 195), as raiders at
  Byblos (EA 122-123) and beside the robbers (EA 318), and as a threat to envoys: Ashur-uballit writes that Pharaoh's
  messengers were delayed because "Suteans had been their pursuers", and he held them until the Suteans were taken
  (EA 16) [A]. The
  steppe road from Mari to Qatna and Damascus crossed their land (see 02, "The Syrian desert") [B].
- <a id="15-ahlamu"></a>**The steppe kept its name for danger** [A, later]: a century on, the Babylonian court excused its silence to Hatti
  by saying the Ahlamu, the steppe's next nomads, made the road impassable, and Hattusili III answered that the excuse
  was false (KBo I 10; Bryce, *Letters*, p. 63: "Is your kingdom so small that (even) the Ahlamu could cut off your
  messengers?") [A, later]; Assyria's kings fought the Suteans and Ahlamu on its western road from Arik-den-ili (about
  1310) on [A, later; C on which king's text first names them] (Grayson, *RIMA* 1).
- <a id="15-shasu"></a>**The Shasu** [A]: Egypt's name for the herdsmen of the land south and east of Canaan (`shasu`, the Negev and Edom);
  Egyptian kings campaigned against them as raiders of the Sinai road (Seti I, year 1, on the road from Tjaru: Breasted,
  *ARE* III, §§83-88), and a later frontier journal records Shasu
  of Edom let past the fortress of Tjeku to water their flocks at the pools of Per-Atum (Papyrus Anastasi VI, about 1200;
  Breasted, *ARE* III, §638: "the tribes of the Shasu of Edom") [A, later].
- **What fed them** [A]: hunger and lords who did not protect (EA 74, 85, 118, 288; see 14). The letters name the
  Habiru wherever a lord's hold was weak and his neighbours at war: Amurru, the land round Byblos, the hills of Shechem
  and Jerusalem [A]. That is the intent's rule ("Banditry and piracy rise where an owner spends little on patrols and
  garrisons, and with poverty, war and disaster").

## Tolls and customs

- <a id="15-duty-free"></a>**A king could waive his dues for a friend** [A]: the king of Alashiya asks Pharaoh that "no one making a claim in
  your name" approach his merchants or his ship (EA 39), and the governor of Alashiya writes the same to his Egyptian
  counterpart for his king's men and ship (EA 40). Moran reads this as a plea for "safe passage and exemption from
  impost" (EA 39, n. 2), as does Bryce (*Letters*, p. 54: "exempted from customs duties") [B], and likens it to
  Mitanni's passport, whose messenger "is to owe nothing" (EA 30). These are the only dues the letters attest, and show
  dues taken on ships arriving in Egypt and on travellers crossing Canaan [B].
- <a id="15-dues"></a>**Where dues were taken** [B]: at a harbour's quay, at a city gate and at the border fort. Mesopotamian quays (*kāru*)
  took a toll (*miksu*) on boats and goods, and later Kassite land grants (*kudurrus*) exempt their holders from it, and
  from feeding the king's messengers, as a royal favour [A, later: the kudurrus of the 13th-12th centuries]. Ugarit's
  harbour at Mahadu had an overseer of the quay, and its texts record dues on ships and goods [B: van Soldt; Monroe].
  An Egyptian frontier post, perhaps Tjaru (Sile) at the head of the Ways of Horus, kept a daybook of the messengers who
  passed it (Papyrus Anastasi III, verso, about 1210; Breasted, *ARE* III, §§629-632: "the names and the business of
  the messengers who passed"; the place is Erman's guess) [A, later].
- <a id="15-toll-rates"></a>**The rates of the period are lost** [S]. The only rates on record are Old Assyrian, five centuries earlier [A,
  earlier]: at Kanesh the local palace took a tax on entry (*nishatum*) of about 2 shekels in 65 on tin (about 3%) and
  5% on textiles, and the cities on the road took transit dues besides, in tin or cloth (Veenhof; Larsen). **Start
  from:** a default rate of 3%, the low end, per border crossed, which a holder may raise or waive as the intent
  says; at 5% the goods of a Babylon-Egypt caravan crossing four or five holders would lose a fifth to tolls, which the
  record of merchants still trading that road does not suggest.
- **Tolls were a political gift as well as revenue** [B]: exemptions were asked for and granted between friends (EA 39;
  the kudurrus), and Hattusili III's rule on the merchants of Ura (RS 17.130) shows an overlord setting who could trade
  where. The intent's rate per partner, zero included, is that [B].

## Smuggling and contraband

- <a id="15-smuggling"></a>**Smuggling is attested, but not in the period** [A, earlier]. Old Assyrian merchants took the "narrow road"
  (*harrān sūqinnim*) round the toll posts, or slipped goods past the palace at Kanesh; the palace confiscated what it
  caught, and the merchants' letters warn each other not to be caught (Veenhof) [A, earlier]. For 1346 the sources are
  silent [S]; the game's smuggling is that old practice, made where tolls are.
- <a id="15-shaushgamuwa-embargo"></a>**Contraband: what kings forbade** [A, later]. Tudhaliya IV's treaty with Shaushgamuwa of Amurru (about 1230) bars
  Amurru's merchants from going to Assyria and Assyria's from entering Amurru, and is read as barring Ahhiyawa's ships
  from Assyria by way of Amurru [A for the embargo; C for the Ahhiyawa line] (Beckman; AhT 2, §13': "Your merchant
  shall not go to Assyria"). Kings also held back their own goods: Hattusili III wrote to an Assyrian king that good iron
  was not to be had in his armoury in Kizzuwatna just then (KBo I 14; Bryce, *Letters*, p. 97) [A, later]. In 1346, what kings asked each other for (gold, horses, chariots, lapis, copper) moved as gifts and trade
  under their seals [A: EA 1-41]; nothing on record was forbidden [S].
- **Reading [G]:** a smuggler in the game is a caravan or ship that crosses a border without paying its toll; policing
  at the border catches some and confiscates the goods, as Kanesh's palace did, and nothing more: no contraband list
  until a treaty makes one (a sealed tablet may say "no trade with X", as Shaushgamuwa's did, and it binds only by
  words).

## Policing

### Egypt: the Medjay, the forts and the garrisons

- <a id="15-medjay"></a>**The Medjay** [A]: once a desert people of the eastern Nubian desert, by the New Kingdom a corps of desert police
  and guards [B]. At Akhetaten their chief Mahu's tomb shows him and his police running before the king's chariot on a
  round of inspection of the guard posts, and bringing three captured men before the vizier (Davies, *Rock Tombs of El
  Amarna* IV: "Mahu and his fifteen police ... feel obliged to run before the chariot") [A]. The patrol roads they kept round the city's desert edge are still
  visible (Kemp; see 12) [A].
- <a id="15-semna"></a>**Border control was old practice** [A, earlier]. Senusret III's boundary stela at Semna (about 1860) forbids any
  Nubian to pass north by water or land except to trade at Iken or on an errand (Breasted, *ARE* I, §652: "to prevent
  that any Negro should cross it, by water or by land"); the Semna Dispatches report the
  fortresses' patrols tracking Medjay in the desert and turning them back [A, earlier] (Smither; Kemp). In 1346 Egypt's
  frontier fortresses on the Ways of Horus (see 12) and in Nubia did the same [B].
- <a id="15-canaan-garrisons"></a>**Garrisons on the roads of Canaan** [A]: Egyptian and Nubian soldiers held Gaza, Joppa (Yapu), Kumidi and Sumur
  [A: Gaza and Joppa EA 289, 294, 296; Sumur EA 62, 67, 76; Kumidi EA 116, 129, 197], while Beth-Shean's garrison was
  men of Gath (EA 289) [A]; Yahtiru writes that he guards "the dry gate of Azzatu and the city gate of Yapu"
  (EA 296, Moran) [A]. Vassals ask for garrisons in tens: Abdi-Heba asks for "50 men as a garrison to protect the land" (EA 289), Biridiya of
  Megiddo for a hundred (EA 244) [A] (see 11, "The city-states"). Vassals fed the king's troops when they passed and
  stocked their grain (EA 324-325) [A].
- <a id="15-kashi"></a>**The police robbed too** [A]. Abdi-Heba's Kashi soldiers attacked him in his own house: "I was almost killed by the
  Kashites in my own house" (EA 287, Moran) [A]; a generation later Horemheb's edict (Kruchten; Breasted, *ARE* III,
  §§45ff.: "his nose shall be cut off, and he shall be sent to Tharu") punishes soldiers and officials who seized boats, hides and produce from the people, with the loss of
  the nose and exile to Tjaru (about 1320) [A, slightly later]. **Reading [G]:** policing has a price in the people's
  goodwill as well as in men; item 15 may leave that to the existing rules on troops quartered in a province, and add
  nothing.

### Hatti: the border governors and the watch

- <a id="15-bel-madgalti"></a>The border governor (*bel madgalti*) kept the forts, roads and gates, posted watchmen and patrols, and judged cases
  in the towns he toured (Miller; see 12, "Roads and their upkeep") [A]. His instructions bid him keep the watch posts
  manned and the scouts out and close the gates at night [A] (Miller). This is the Hittite form of
  item 15's policing.
- <a id="15-fugitives"></a>**Fugitives** [A]: nearly every Hittite treaty has a clause for returning fugitives who cross the border
  (Beckman: Sunashshura of Kizzuwatna, a generation or more before 1346, and Huqqana of Hayasa, under Suppiluliuma)
  [A]. Runaways are the Habiru's source
  (see 14), so a king who returns them takes men from the road's bands [B]. **Reading [G]:** no rule; a sealed tablet
  may say it, and it binds by words.

### Ugarit and the merchant towns

- <a id="15-tamkar-sharri"></a>Ugarit kept the king's merchants (*tamkār sharri*), who traded abroad under his name and on his credit, and his
  officials answered for foreign merchants in his land under the overlord's edicts (above) [A, later; B for 1346]
  (van Soldt; Monroe). No police corps of Ugarit is named [S].

### What policing cost

- <a id="15-policing-cost"></a>**No court counts it** [S]. What is on record is size, not cost: tens of soldiers in a vassal town (EA 289, 244),
  a corps of Medjay at the capital under one chief (Mahu's tomb; their numbers unknown [S]), a chain of about twenty
  forts on the Ways of Horus about 1290 (see 12) [A, later].
- **How it was paid** [B]: soldiers in rations and land (see 11, "Pay"), garrisons fed by the town they guarded
  (EA 324-325), the Medjay as state servants on rations [B: Deir el-Medina's Medjay, later, drew rations like the
  workmen]. **Reading [G]:** policing is paid as everything built is ("Paid in gold or in labour"), in men taken from
  the province's levy and their food, with gold as the alternative.
- <a id="15-police-start"></a>**Start from** [G]: a province's police at the low end of a garrison's range, **20 men for a province on a main road
  and 50 for one on the frontier or in bandit country**, the size of the garrisons the vassals asked for (EA 289); each
  holds banditry down in that province only, and costs its men's rations as soldiers in garrison do (see 11). Those are
  first guesses for item 18 to tune.

## What item 15 should not read into this

- **No rule names a people or a coast.** The Lukka, the Habiru and the Suteans are where banditry and piracy start,
  from poverty, war and weak lords, as the intent's rule gives it; the same rule makes outlaws anywhere those
  conditions hold ("Different data, same rules").
- **The grievances are one side's word.** Burna-Buriash wanted silver, Abdi-Heba troops, Rib-Hadda ships; what they
  show is what a king hears, not what happened.
- **Later rules are norms, not engines.** Ugarit's edicts and the Hittite laws show what was thought fair; in the game
  they are what a king may demand in words, not a payment the engine makes ("Style, not strategy").
