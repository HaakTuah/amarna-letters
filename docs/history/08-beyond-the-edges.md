# 8. Beyond the edges: the peoples newcomers come from

For "Arrivals come through doors", "Who they are" and "Who speaks what" (item 11), and the edge markets (item 9+).
Grades and abbreviations: [README](README.md).

**In one line:** the doors are the western sea (Keftiu and Ahhiyawa), the eastern road beyond Susa, the Gulf (Magan),
the Nile beyond Nubia and, once added, the Red Sea (Punt); most newcomers come from just beyond the edge, few from far,
and each door brings its own mix of names, tongues and reasons.

**Candidates the sources suggest, not doors in the design** (design questions): **the Libyan desert** west of the Delta
(the Tjehenu and Tjemehu, raiders and later invaders of Egypt; Libyans already appear in Egyptian reliefs of the 18th
Dynasty [A]), and **the north-eastern highlands** beyond Isuwa (Azzi-Hayasa, ruled by Hukkana, Suppiluliuma's
brother-in-law [A]). Both lie beyond lands the map reaches; the reading taken is to leave them out until item 11 asks
whether Egypt's and Hatti's doors are starved without them.

**First guesses for each door's share of arrivals [G],** before item 11a's traffic: the western sea 35%, the Nile
beyond Nubia 25%, the eastern road 25%, the Gulf 10%, the Red Sea 5% (if added; otherwise shared among the rest). By
distance, as "Who they are" says: of the western sea's men, nine in ten are from the Aegean's near shores; of the
eastern road's, nine in ten from Elam's highlands and the Zagros; **one arrival in a hundred** is from the tin lands,
across Iran [G].

## The peoples, one by one

- **Ahhiyawa** (the Mycenaean Greeks). Known from Hittite texts from the late 15th century on (the Madduwatta text) and
  most prominent in the 13th; a Great King in one Hittite text of the 13th century [A]. Linear B names for their men
  ("Name styles" in the people dossier). Language: Mycenaean Greek [A].
- **Keftiu** (Crete). Theban tomb paintings (Rekhmire, Senenmut) show Keftiu bringing gifts; Amenhotep III's statue
  base at Kom el-Hetan lists Knossos, Amnisos, Phaistos, Kydonia, Mycenae and Kythera [A]. In 1346 Knossos is probably under
  Greek rulers writing Linear B [C: the dates of the tablets and of the palace's fall are argued]. Keftiu names survive in an Egyptian school exercise, "names of the Keftiu" [A];
  most newcomers take Linear B names [B].
- **Sherden** (*shirdanu*). Fighting men at Byblos in Rib-Hadda's letters (EA 81, 122-123) [A]; later Ramesses II's
  guard. Their homeland is unknown (Sardinia is a later guess) [C]. Names: none survive, invented and marked [G].
- **Lukka.** Raiders of Alashiya year by year (EA 38) [A]; on the map as a lawless coast, so their men come from the
  crowd ("Who they are").
- **Danuna.** A land named in a letter from Tyre (EA 151) [A], probably in Cilicia or the Levant's north [C]; on or
  near the map, so from the crowd or the nearest door.
- **The later Sea Peoples:** Shekelesh, Teresh, Peleset, Tjeker, Weshesh, and Ekwesh, first named under Merneptah
  (about 1208) and Ramesses III (about 1177), 140 to 170 years after 1346 [A for the dates]. They come rarely, as
  adventurers and soldiers for hire under their later names, marked in the data as first recorded later ("Who they
  are").
- **Elamites and the highlanders.** Elam's kings at Susa and Anshan [A]; the Lullubi and Qutu of the Zagros, raiders
  in Mesopotamian memory [A]. Elamite names ("Name styles"); highlanders' names few, invented and marked [G].
- **The tin lands.** Somewhere in Central Asia (Mušiston in the Zeravshan valley is one proposed source; Powell et al.
  2022 [C]) and perhaps Afghanistan; no text of the period names them [A: the silence]. He speaks nothing anyone knows
  ("Who they are").
- **Dilmun and Magan.** Dilmun was Babylonia's in 1346 (a Kassite governor) [B]; Magan, its copper trade past its
  height, was known by name [A]. Their languages are unknown [C].
- **Meluhha.** In the third millennium the Indus; in the Amarna letters, the name for Nubia (EA 70, 95, 108, 112, 117,
  127, 131-133) [B: an identification]. The Indus cities were gone by 1346 [A], so nobody comes from there.
- **Kush, Irem and the Medjay.** Beyond Egypt's frontier at the Fourth Cataract lay Irem and other lands Egypt raided
  and traded with [B]; the Medjay, once desert nomads of the east, had become Egypt's police by name [B]. Names: Nubian,
  mostly unknown; Egyptian names among those who served Egypt [B].
- **Punt.** Hatshepsut's expedition (about 1470) is the great record [A]; under Akhenaten no voyage is attested [S].

## What each door's men know of a map language

As "Who speaks what" decides, by reason for coming: a trader or a soldier for hire who has come this way before has
some of the language spoken where his door opens; a wanderer, a refugee or a man from the tin lands has none; a local
coming home is fluent in his own. What the sources add [B]:

| Door | The map language a returning trader or soldier knows | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The western sea | Akkadian (broken) at Ugarit and Alashiya; Luwian on Arzawa's coast; Egyptian in the Delta | Aegean traders worked through Ugarit's and Cyprus's merchants |
| The eastern road | Akkadian | Elam wrote Akkadian; the road ran into Babylonia |
| The Gulf | Akkadian | Dilmun was Babylonian |
| The Nile beyond Nubia | Egyptian | Egypt's soldiers and traders in the south |
| The Red Sea | Egyptian | Only Egypt sailed there |
