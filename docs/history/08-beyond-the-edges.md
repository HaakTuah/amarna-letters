# 8. Beyond the edges: the peoples newcomers come from

For "Arrivals come through doors", "Who they are" and "Who speaks what" (item 11), and the edge markets (item 9+).
Grades and abbreviations: [README](README.md).

**In one line:** the doors are the western sea (Keftiu and Ahhiyawa), the eastern road beyond Susa, the Gulf (Magan),
the Nile beyond Nubia and, once added, the Red Sea (Punt); most newcomers come from just beyond the edge, few from far,
and each door brings its own mix of names, tongues and reasons.

**Candidates the sources suggest, not doors in the design** (design questions): **the Libyan desert** west of the Delta
(the Tjehenu and Tjemehu, raiders and later invaders of Egypt; Libyans already appear in Egyptian reliefs of the 18th
Dynasty [A]), and **the north-eastern highlands** beyond Isuwa (Azzi-Hayasa, ruled by Hukkana, Suppiluliuma's
brother-in-law [A]). Both lie beyond lands the map reaches; the reading taken is to leave them out until item 11 asks
whether Egypt's and Hatti's doors are starved without them.

**First guesses for each door's share of arrivals [G],** before item 11a's traffic: the western sea 35%, the Nile
beyond Nubia 25%, the eastern road 25%, the Gulf 10%, the Red Sea 5% (if added; otherwise shared among the rest). By
distance, as "Who they are" says: of the western sea's men, nine in ten are from the Aegean's near shores; of the
eastern road's, nine in ten from Elam's highlands and the Zagros; **one arrival in a hundred** is from the tin lands,
across Iran [G].

## The peoples, one by one

- <a id="08-ahhiyawa"></a>**Ahhiyawa** (the Mycenaean Greeks). Known from Hittite texts from the late 15th or early 14th
  century on (an oracle text and the Madduwatta text, as "Ahhiya") and most prominent in the 13th; called "Great King"
  and "my brother" by a Hittite king in the Tawagalawa letter of the 13th century [A] (Beckman, Bryce and Cline, *The
  Ahhiyawa Texts*, 2011, p. 97: "our earliest attestation in Hittite records of the name Ahhiyawa"; p. 122: "his
  Ahhiyawan counterpart as a 'Great King,' and as 'my brother'"). Linear B names for their men ("Name styles" in the
  people dossier). Language: Mycenaean Greek [A].
- <a id="08-keftiu"></a>**Keftiu** (Crete). Theban tomb paintings (Rekhmire, Senenmut) show Keftiu bringing gifts
  (Breasted, *ARE* II, §761, Rekhmire: "the tribute of Keftyew"); Amenhotep III's statue base at Kom el-Hetan lists
  Knossos, Amnisos, Phaistos, Kydonia, Mycenae and Kythera [A, as read by Cline and Stannish 2011, pp. 6-9, Phaistos and
  Kydonia recarved]. In 1346 Knossos is probably under Greek rulers writing Linear B [C: the dates of the tablets and of
  the palace's fall are argued]. Keftiu names survive in an Egyptian school exercise, "names of the Keftiu" [A]; most
  newcomers take Linear B names [G].
- <a id="08-sherden"></a>**Sherden** (*shirdanu*). Men called *širdanu* at Byblos in Rib-Hadda's letters (EA 81,
  122-123) [A]; whether they are the Sherden of Egyptian texts, and fighting men, is argued [C] (Moran, glossary: "a
  term that probably has nothing to do with" them, "A type of soldier?"; Rainey, EA 122: "they killed a Sherdanu").
  Later Ramesses II's guard. Their homeland is unknown (Sardinia is one modern proposal) [C]. Names: invented and marked
  [G].
- <a id="08-lukka"></a>**Lukka.** Raiders of Alashiya year by year (EA 38) [A] (Moran: "men of Lukki, year by year,
  seize villages in my own country"); on the map as a lawless coast, so their men come from the crowd ("Who they are").
- <a id="08-danuna"></a>**Danuna.** A land named in a letter from Tyre (EA 151) [A] (Moran: "The king of Danuna died"),
  probably in Cilicia or the Levant's north [C] (Moran, glossary: "a kingdom in eastern Cilicia"); on or near the map,
  so from the crowd or the nearest door.
- <a id="08-sea-peoples"></a>**The later Sea Peoples:** Shekelesh, Teresh, Peleset, Tjeker, Weshesh, and Ekwesh, first
  named under Merneptah (about 1208) and Ramesses III (about 1177), 140 to 170 years after 1346 [A for the dates]. They
  come rarely, as adventurers and soldiers for hire under their later names, marked in the data as first recorded later
  ("Who they are").
- <a id="08-elam"></a>**Elamites and the highlanders.** Elam's kings at Susa and Anshan [A]; the Lullubi and Qutu of the
  Zagros, raiders in Mesopotamian memory [A]. Elamite names ("Name styles"); highlanders' names few, invented and marked
  [G].
- <a id="08-tin-lands"></a>**The tin lands.** Somewhere in Central Asia (Mušiston in the Zeravshan valley is one
  proposed source; Powell et al. 2022: "ores from Central Asia (Uzbekistan and Tajikistan)" for a third of the Uluburun
  tin [C]) and perhaps Afghanistan (Berger et al. 2023: "could also originate from Afghanistan"; they favour south-west
  England for much of it [C]); no text of the period names them [S]. He speaks nothing anyone knows ("Who they are").
- <a id="08-dilmun-and-magan"></a>**Dilmun and Magan.** Dilmun was Babylonia's in 1346 (a Kassite official there, in the
  Ili-ippashra letters from Nippur) [B]; Magan, the copper land of Mesopotamian texts from about 2300 on [A], is named
  in no text of 1346 the dossiers use [S]. Their languages are unknown [S].
- <a id="08-meluhha"></a>**Meluhha.** In the third millennium the Indus; in the Amarna letters, the name for Nubia (EA
  70, 95, 108, 112, 117, 132, 133; Kaši, Cush, in the same requests in EA 127 and 131, and as Meluhha's gloss in EA 133)
  [B: an identification] (Moran, EA 70, n. 2: "'Meluhha' designated Nubia"). The Indus cities were gone by 1346 [B; no
  source checked], so nobody comes from there.
- <a id="08-kush-irem-medjay"></a>**Kush, Irem and the Medjay.** Beyond Egypt's frontier at the Fourth Cataract lay Irem
  and other lands Egypt raided and traded with [B]; the Medjay, once desert nomads of the east, had become Egypt's
  police by name [B]. Names: Nubian, mostly unknown; Egyptian names among those who served Egypt [B].
- <a id="08-punt"></a>**Punt.** Hatshepsut's expedition (about 1470) is the great record [A] (Breasted, *ARE* II, §246:
  "successfully concluded just before her ninth year"); under Akhenaten no voyage is attested [S].

## What each door's men know of a map language

As "Who speaks what" decides, by reason for coming: a trader or a soldier for hire who has come this way before has
some of the language spoken where his door opens; a wanderer, a refugee or a man from the tin lands has none; a local
coming home is fluent in his own. What the sources add [B]:

| Door | The map language a returning trader or soldier knows | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The western sea | Akkadian (broken) at Ugarit and Alashiya; Luwian on Arzawa's coast; Egyptian in the Delta | Aegean traders worked through Ugarit's and Cyprus's merchants |
| The eastern road | Akkadian | Elam wrote Akkadian; the road ran into Babylonia |
| The Gulf | Akkadian | Dilmun was Babylonian |
| The Nile beyond Nubia | Egyptian | Egypt's soldiers and traders in the south |
| The Red Sea | Egyptian | Only Egypt sailed there |
