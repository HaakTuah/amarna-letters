# 3. Environment and seasons

For "Land and climate", "Rivers" and "Seasons" (travel's seasons in item 7; fields, rivers and winter supplies in item
10+; the economy's harvests in item 9+). Grades and abbreviations: [README](README.md).

**In one line:** two farming worlds share the board: the flood-irrigated valleys (Egypt and Nubia on the Nile, Babylonia
on the Euphrates and Tigris) and the rain-fed lands (the Levant, north Syria, Assyria, Anatolia, Cyprus), and their
calendars differ; Egypt's three seasons fit them all as four-month blocks, since in 1346 the civil calendar ran within
a week or two of the real year, by the reckonings below ("Seasons").

<a id="03-calendar"></a>The game's seasons, as months of the real year (Egyptian civil calendar near its alignment) [B]:
**Akhet** about mid-July to mid-November, **Peret** mid-November to mid-March, **Shemu** mid-March to mid-July. When the
civil New Year last fell on Sirius's rising is a reckoning, not a record [C]: by Theon of Alexandria's count (ca. 335-405
AD) a Sothic cycle began in 1322 BC, so 1346 was some 6 days off; HKW, p. 443: "would have begun, in our terms, in 1322
BC". Krauss warns that a cycle's start reckoned back schematically "is astronomically incorrect by several days" (p.
444), and on his own reconstruction "the cycle would not have started in 1322 BC ... but 10 x 4 years — 40 years later"
(p. 445), which puts 1346 some 16 days off. Either way the four-month blocks hold to within a fortnight. The seasons,
HKW, p. 47: "Akhet ... 'inundation'; Peret ... 'winter' ... Shemu ... 'summer'".

<a id="03-hard-winters"></a>**Hard winters** (for "Winter in the field"): the Anatolian plateau and the Taurus passes are hardest; inland Syria,
the Habur and Assyria are cold with frost; the coasts, Egypt and Babylonia are mild [B]. The Hittite kings campaigned
from spring to autumn and came home for the winter [B]: Suppiluliuma went home from Carchemish "before the winter
snows set in" (Bryce, Letters, p. 180), and Mursili II's *Annals* close his tenth year with "I spent the winter in
Hattusas" [A] ([EIEOL, Hittite lesson 5](https://lrc.la.utexas.edu/eieol/hitol/50)).

## Rivers and floods

- <a id="03-nile-flood"></a>**The Nile** begins to rise at the start of June, peaks in September (late September to
  early October at Cairo) and then falls; its height decides Egypt's harvest [B]: HKW, pp. 370-371 (Krauss): "towards
  the end of May and the beginning of June it began to rise", "At Roda the maximum height was reached between
  September 25 and October 5". A low flood means hunger [B: the Ptolemaic Famine Stela's seven years without the flood,
  [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Famine_Stela)]; a very high one floods the land [A]: HKW, p. 372:
  "Osorkon III, year 3 ... flooding of Karnak temple". How often: the medieval Roda nilometer record suggests a poor
  flood about one year in five and a disastrous one rarely, perhaps once a generation [C: Hassan's studies of the Roda record, not reached; the pages found give runs of bad years, not a rate: "From between 1052 and 1090 there were a total of 28 low floods" (Penn State, EARTH 105, lesson 8)]. No bad flood is recorded
  for Akhenaten's reign [S]. **First guess [G]:** a low Nile one Akhet in eight, a ruinous one one in thirty.
- <a id="03-tigris-euphrates-floods"></a>**The Tigris and the Euphrates** rise with the snowmelt in the Taurus and
  Zagros, the Tigris peaking in April and the Euphrates in May, at or just before the harvest [B:
  [Wikipedia, Agriculture in Mesopotamia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agriculture_in_Mesopotamia), citing Sanlaville
  2000: "in April for the Tigris and in May for the Euphrates"], so their floods were a danger the works were built against [B: the same page:
  "The irrigation system was also designed to limit the risk of floods, by means of basins that could retain excess
  water and canals that could drain it away, as well as dams"]; that a high spring flood breaks dykes is the game's
  reading [G]. A broken dam is attested, but from its owner's neglect, not the season [A: Laws of Hammurabi § 53,
  [Avalon](https://avalon.law.yale.edu/ancient/hamcode.asp): "If any one be too lazy to keep his dam in proper
  condition, and does not so keep it; if then the dam break and all the fields be flooded"]. Salt was the south's worst problem [B: Wikipedia, Agriculture in Mesopotamia, citing Huot 1989: "the salinisation of
  the soil"]. **First guess [G]:** a damaging flood one Shemu in six on each river.
- **The Orontes, Habur, Balikh and Halys** are smaller rivers with spring maxima; not navigable for trade [B].

## The sea

- <a id="03-sailing-season"></a>**Sailing season:** roughly April to October; winter storms and short days close the
  open sea from about November to March [B: later Greek and Roman evidence: Vegetius 4.39, "Ex die ... tertio idus
  Nouembres usque in diem sextum idus Martias maria clauduntur" (11 November to 10 March); Hesiod, *Works and Days*
  663-665 (Evelyn-White): "Fifty days after the solstice ... is the right time"]. Coasting goes on longer
  in fair spells [G]. This is Peret = closed, as "Seasons" says.
- **Winds:** north and north-west winds prevail in summer (the Etesians, about mid-May to mid-September in the Aegean:
  [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Etesian)), helping ships south to Egypt
  and slowing them north; so the circuit ran anticlockwise ("The sea" in the geography dossier) [B].
- **Currents:** an anticlockwise current runs along the Levantine coast northwards [B: E. Marcus, *Maritime Trade in the Southern Levant* (D.Phil., Oxford, 1998), p. 95: "The Coriolis effect explains the counterclockwise movement of fluids in the Earth's northern hemisphere, creating the longshore current in the Mediterranean", which "varies from one-quarter to one and one-quarter knots"].

**Readings for the game:** 1346 is an ordinary climate, before the drying that preceded the collapse of about 1200,
so droughts and plagues are occasional events rather than a trend [B]. Rain-fed lands (Hatti above all, then the
Levant, Mitanni and Assyria) suffer drought; flood lands suffer bad floods; trading ports and besieged cities catch
plague, as the letters show. **First guesses [G]:** a drought on the Anatolian plateau one year in six, in the Levant
and north Syria one in eight; a plague in a port or a besieged city one season in a hundred per province, more in a
siege.
